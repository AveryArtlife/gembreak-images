## The single principle that governs everything

> **The consumer's *experience* can be pure retail. The prescribing *decision* must be real medicine. Keep those separate and you're Hims. Blur them and you're a pill mill.**

Everything about the storefront — the browsing, the branding, the "add to cart" feel, the transparent pricing, the fast checkout, the subscription — is UX you can optimize as aggressively as any ecommerce brand. The law doesn't require your *website* to feel like a doctor's office. It requires the *prescriber's decision* to be a genuine clinical judgment. That's the whole game, and it's a much lighter constraint than it sounds — it costs you a real video visit and a real (nonzero) decline rate, and buys you a legal, bankable, advertisable business.

## Why your video instinct is the smart move (not just the compliant one)

You offered video. Take it — and lean all the way in, because it's a genuine legal edge:

- **Most states require synchronous audio-visual contact to establish a prescribing relationship.** Video satisfies the widest set of state laws at once, so it's the most *portable* model as you expand nationally.
- **Peptides aren't controlled substances**, so the federal in-person-exam requirement (Ryan Haight) doesn't apply — a video visit is legally sufficient to prescribe them. You get the legitimacy of a real visit without the in-person burden.
- **Here's the strategic part:** Hims's *async questionnaire* model is the one under fire. A Senate investigation flagged direct-to-consumer telehealth for "high" prescription rates (one program: 100% of visits resulted in the drug), and the FDA has cracked down on telehealth ad practices. **By running real video visits with genuine clinical judgment, you're adopting the *more* defensible posture than the category leader** — exactly what you want when you're smaller and prescribing gray-zone compounds. Your competitors' corner-cutting is your compliance moat.

So: video isn't the tax on this model. It's the feature that makes prescribing BPC-157-class peptides defensible.

## The rubber-stamp trap — the one thing that decides legal vs. not

This is the "most legal version" answer, stated plainly. The difference between a legitimate video-Rx retail business and an illegal one dressed up with a doctor is a single question:

> **Can the doctor say no — and do they, sometimes, actually say no?**

A video visit where the provider approves 100% of orders, can't decline, and is just confirming what the consumer already put in their cart is **theater**, and a medical board will call it what it is. The Senate literally flagged the 100%-approval pattern as the red flag. The refund-on-decline mechanic Hims/Ro use exists precisely so declines are operationally normal.

The design rule that follows is counterintuitive but it's your shield: **build the funnel so that declining or modifying is a normal, comfortable outcome, not a broken checkout.** Concretely —

- The provider has real authority and a real protocol for when to decline or modify (contraindications, red-flag history, inappropriate requests, labs out of range).
- Declined/modified consults get a full refund and an alternative recommendation (a cosmetic product, a different peptide, "see your PCP first"). Nobody feels robbed; you stay legitimate.
- **You track your decline/modify rate as a core metric.** A nonzero rate is the single best piece of evidence, if a board ever asks, that this is real medicine. If it's 0%, you don't have a practice — you have a liability. Somewhere in the low-double-digits is what "a real doctor is actually reviewing these" looks like.

This isn't me softening the model — it *is* the most-legal version. The realness of the gate is the entire thing you're buying by having a doctor.

## The storefront design: two lanes, one retail experience

The trick to keeping it retail-feeling is running two fulfillment lanes behind one beautiful storefront:

**Lane 1 — True retail (add to cart, ship): cosmetic + supplement SKUs.**
GHK-Cu / Matrixyl / Argireline serums, collagen, legal precursors. No gate, no visit, pure ecommerce. This is your top-of-funnel, your cash flow, your ad-friendly surface, and the thing a consumer can buy in 60 seconds. (Details in the earlier docs.)

**Lane 2 — Retail-feel, Rx-gated: the peptide protocols.**
The consumer browses these exactly like products — photos, descriptions, transparent monthly pricing, reviews — but the button says **"Start Visit"** or **"Check Eligibility,"** not "Add to Cart." The flow:

1. Consumer selects the peptide/protocol they want (retail feel: they're "shopping").
2. Structured medical intake + ID verification (feels like a smart checkout).
3. Labs where standard of care indicates ⚖️ (tiered — required for some protocols, lighter for lower-risk ones; your doctor sets the protocol).
4. **Synchronous video visit** with a provider licensed in the patient's state. Real evaluation, real judgment, can decline/modify.
5. If appropriate → e-prescribed (Photon) to your named 503A pharmacy → compounded against a COA'd batch → shipped.
6. Subscription for refills + structured follow-up visits (retention + ongoing-relationship evidence).

To the consumer this feels like: *"I picked what I wanted, did a quick video with a doctor, and it showed up."* That's retail. To the law it's: *bona fide relationship, synchronous evaluation, individualized prescription, licensed pharmacy.* Both true.

**Framing discipline ⚖️:** on Lane 2, language is "request a consultation for" / "if appropriate, your provider may prescribe" — never "buy peptides." The consumer is purchasing a medical service that may result in a prescription, not buying a drug off a shelf. This wording is not cosmetic; it's what keeps the storefront on the right side of "offering an unapproved drug for sale."

## The structure that holds it together ⚖️

- **MSO/PC split:** you own the MSO (storefront, brand, marketing, tech, logistics); your physician owns the PC (the practice, the prescribing, the clinical protocols, provider hiring). MSO provides non-clinical services to PC under contract. This is the corporate-practice-of-medicine firewall — and your real doctor makes it clean instead of a sham.
- **Licensure map:** the doctor must be licensed in each patient's state. Launch in the states your doctor holds (or can quickly get) licenses in; expand by adding licenses or contracting in-state providers. Geo-gate Lane 2 visibility to licensed states.
- **Named 503A pharmacy** (two, dual-sourced), COA per batch feeding your public `/labs/{batch-id}` transparency pages — compliance as brand.
- **Real EHR** (Healthie) — every visit, decision, and rationale documented and auditable. Your records are your defense.
- **Malpractice coverage** ⚖️ explicitly covering compounded-peptide telehealth (many policies exclude it — confirm in writing).

## The honest risks (so you go in clear-eyed)

- **DTC telehealth is under active scrutiny.** A Senate report criticized the DTC-telehealth prescribing model; the FDA has gone after telehealth ad practices. Running the *defensible* version (video, real declines, no overreaching claims) is how you avoid being the example they make.
- **BPC-157 and the pending five are genuinely unsettled.** You're taking real regulatory risk. The gate is the defense, not a guarantee.
- **Your doctor is personally exposed** — their license is the asset. They need their own counsel and the right malpractice policy. (Full risk brief in the physician-anchored doc.)
- **Billing/subscription complaints sink telehealth brands' reputations** (Hims's most common complaints are billing, not medical). Make cancellation easy and billing transparent — it's cheap insurance.
