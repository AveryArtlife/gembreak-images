Read this before you trust a single line of the table. You were right that names such as halolab.com and mitoboost.com are not available, and it is worth being precise about why the old list said they were. Both still answer NXDOMAIN at public DNS today — they are genuinely absent from the .com zone — yet they are registered. A domain that is registered but has no nameservers set (parked without DNS, held by an investor, or inside a registrar's own inventory) is invisible to a DNS check. DNS can prove a domain is taken; it cannot prove one is free. The authoritative sources that could — RDAP, WHOIS on port 43, and every registrar API — are all blocked by this environment's network policy, which I tested again today. So this document does two things it can do honestly: it removes every name DNS proves is gone, and it ranks what remains by strength, so you can walk the list from the top and buy the first one your registrar confirms. The right-hand column grades how much to trust each NXDOMAIN, and short names get the lowest grade precisely because names like Halo Lab are the ones people quietly hold.

The single-word .com game is over. I generated 2,589 fresh short coined .coms (5–8 characters, pronounceable patterns) and tested every one: 2,079 were already delegated, and almost everything left was unpronounceable residue — aevayx, myoyx, nerayx. Names are open in that range because they are bad. Of 323 real English words tested on .com, three came back NXDOMAIN, and all three read like held assets rather than free ones. Plan on a compound .com, or on an alternative TLD.

How each name was scored

five criteria, weighted, applied to every candidate independently
CriterionWeightWhat earns a high score
Brevity25%Characters before the dot. Six or fewer scores full marks; every two characters past eight costs roughly a point and a half.
Brandability25%One word beats two. Two syllables beat four. Penalties for mechanical prefixes, ambiguous spellings that fail a radio test (ryze, thryv, vyv), and consonant pile-ups.
Meaning20%Does it carry real semantic payload — vitality, recovery, clarity, strength, growth — rather than being a shape that happens to be free?
Category fit15%Rewards the lawful supplement nouns (amino, formula, protocol). Heavy penalties for grey-market suffixes and for fat-loss verbs, which are a claim on their face.
Trademark strength15%Arbitrary and coined beat descriptive. Generic category nouns and known live-brand collisions are marked down hard.
